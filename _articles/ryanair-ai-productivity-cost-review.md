An AI savings claim needs a line connecting the tool to a completed piece of work. Ryanair’s latest cost review has named the intended areas of work, but has not yet supplied that line.

The airline’s [November 2 half-year statement](https://corporate.ryanair.com/news/ryanair-h1-pat-falls-15-to-e2-15bn-as-unhedged-oil-doubles-traffic-grows-6-at-lower-fares/) includes increased AI use to improve operational and back-office or support productivity. It places that proposal alongside airport negotiations, possible temporary pay freezes under maturing labor agreements, and lower crewing ratios. These are several measures under review; the statement does not establish that AI has replaced flight crew.

The pressure comes from fuel. Ryanair estimates an approximately €2 billion increase in its fuel bill for the financial year ending March 2028, based on current forward prices. It says higher fares are unlikely to offset that increase fully. The release assigns no separate savings target, implementation schedule or measured return to the proposed AI work.

The budget has two separate questions: how much costs are rising, and what an AI system can change. A large exposure creates urgency, but it does not identify the activities an agent can handle or the capacity it will free. The fuel forecast cannot be carried across into an AI benefits column.

For a support workflow, a credible business case would identify the cases handled, the time spent per accepted resolution, the cost of exceptions and the service quality customers receive. A faster first response could still leave staff doing more repair work later. Those later costs belong in the comparison.

The human consequence also needs an owner. If automation changes a role, someone must decide what work remains and who handles the difficult cases. Ryanair’s announcement is a concrete signal of management intent. Turning it into a productivity result will require evidence from the operations it changes.

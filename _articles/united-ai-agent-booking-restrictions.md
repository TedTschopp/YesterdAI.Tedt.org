An employee can approve a trip and still hand the booking to a tool the airline hasn’t authorized. On Nov. 11, United Airlines said it was taking steps to restrict unauthorized third-party personal AI assistants. The change reaches United’s own digital channels and the travel agencies and aggregators that distribute its flights. United said it was updating its booking, ticketing and distribution policies. [United’s announcement](https://united.mediaroom.com/news-releases?item=125496)

United pointed to two ways delegation can go wrong. Sharing a MileagePlus login can expose account details, and a tool with access can make an unwanted purchase or spend miles. The airline also pointed to check-in, when a traveler acknowledges that their bags contain no prohibited items. [CBS News’ reporting](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/united-airlines-bans-ai-agents-booking-flights/)

The agency distinction matters for corporate travel. Business Travel News reported that United told corporate customers it bars accredited agencies from supplying its content to personal AI agents for search, booking or servicing. An accredited agency needs specific permission for an agent it owns and controls. [Business Travel News’ account of the notice](https://www.businesstravelnews.com/transportation/air/united-restricts-unauthorized-third-party-ai-agent-access)

Ask where a proposed travel assistant hands off the trip. An assistant that builds an itinerary from permitted information can send the employee to an approved booking channel. The employee can then check the fare and payment method and personally acknowledge the check-in requirements.

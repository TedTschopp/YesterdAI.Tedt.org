Buying a company’s shares and choosing its directors can be very different things. Anthropic’s proposed public offering puts that distinction on the cover.

The company [filed an S-1 registration statement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm) on October 26 for an initial public offering of Class A common stock. The preliminary prospectus leaves the number of shares and price range blank. It says Anthropic has applied to list on Nasdaq under ANT. Those are proposed offering terms and an application, rather than a completed sale or listing.

The filing describes a Long-Term Benefit Trust whose Class T shares generally let it elect and remove directors constituting a majority of the authorized board. Those shares have no economic rights. Separately, a founder-controlled entity would hold a Class F share carrying 50.1% of voting power on matters submitted to shareholders generally, including election of directors outside the trust’s class. Class A shares carry one vote each. The trust’s board authority and the founders’ shareholder voting authority operate through different mechanisms.

For enterprise buyers, this creates a useful supplier-review question: which body can make the decision that matters to your service? A public offering can expand the available disclosure record while preserving special rights over corporate direction. A familiar exchange listing does not answer that question by itself.

Read the governance provisions alongside the service agreement, escalation route and commitments your organization actually relies on. Keep proposed arrangements separate from provisions that become final, and track amendments through the [filing record](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/0001628280-26-067168-index.htm). The practical task is to identify who holds authority, what they have committed to provide, and where your organization has a remedy if that commitment is missed.

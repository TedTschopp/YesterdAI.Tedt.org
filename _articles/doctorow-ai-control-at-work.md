An AI proposal can change who decides how work gets done. In a [new essay about Taylorism and AI](https://pluralistic.net/2026/11/27/taylor-made/), Cory Doctorow argues that the promise of tighter control over employees can sell a system even when its business case is weak.

Doctorow uses his account of scientific management as the comparison. Managers buy a method for directing workers from above, while the people doing the job lose influence over how it is organized. He argues that some AI sales pitches appeal to the same desire: fewer employees whose judgment can obstruct a manager’s plans.

His argument leaves room for useful automation. He says workers can benefit from statistical tools when they help decide how those tools enter the job and retain enough time to catch mistakes. The distinction is about who shapes the workflow and bears its consequences.

This is a polemical essay, rather than a controlled deployment study. It provides no measured estimate of how often that sales mechanism explains an AI purchase. Its account of managerial motives should remain attributed to Doctorow.

For an adoption decision, the argument suggests a concrete question: which choices will the people doing the work retain? A proposal can answer by showing who may reject an unsuitable output, change the workflow or stop a failing automation. Those decisions deserve a place beside the projected labor saving.

In a pilot, compare completed work and time spent correcting it. Let workers identify where an apparently faster process moves checking into an uncounted part of their day. Record their objections before deciding whether the system improved the job.

A dashboard may make activity easier for management to see. The person accountable for finishing the task also needs enough authority and time to correct what the dashboard misses.

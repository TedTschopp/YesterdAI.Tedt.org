A faster specialist can still spend the afternoon waiting for someone else’s answer. The same problem belongs in an assessment of AI agents.

In an [October 29 DeepMind Institute essay](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/the-economics-of-intelligence/), Alex Imas, Andrew Koh and Shane Legg separate two capabilities: doing a task and coordinating the people or agents who do the work. Their economic framework argues that improving the first can raise productivity while leaving firms broadly recognizable. Improving coordination past a critical threshold could change how large firms can become and how markets organize.

The mechanism is division of labor. A team can combine complementary strengths and move work between specialists. Copying one model adds capacity, but copies may share the same weaknesses. A strong individual score therefore leaves open whether a group can divide an unfamiliar problem, exchange useful context and catch mistakes before they spread.

These are conditional economic scenarios, not evidence that an agent organization has crossed such a threshold. The essay itself says today’s AI systems coordinate poorly in practice. The Institute also identifies its pieces as authors’ ideas and research, rather than Google’s official position. A forecast about concentration or wages should retain those qualifications.

For an enterprise, the useful next step is a workflow comparison. Give a single agent and a small agent team the same bounded job, tools and acceptance criteria. Keep a record of duplicated effort, lost context, correction work, elapsed time and the result a person actually accepts. Then increase the team size and see whether useful output grows faster than the coordination burden.

That comparison can expose a very ordinary bottleneck: several capable agents waiting, repeating or repairing one another’s work. Before buying a larger team, find out whether it can hand over a job cleanly.
